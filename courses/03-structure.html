Structure

Almost everybody writes in the order they figured things out: here is the background, here is what happened, here is what I concluded, here is what I need. That is the order of your thinking and it is exactly backwards for a reader, who is deciding in the first sentence whether this is theirs to deal with. Tonight: put the ask on top, learn three skeletons that cover almost everything, and get a number on how buried your ask currently is. Halfway scorecard at the end.

Part 1 · covers order

You wrote it in the order you worked it out

6 min live

That order is honest, it is chronological, and it is the reason the ask ends up at the bottom. The reader is not retracing your investigation. They are deciding, in about one sentence, whether this is theirs.

LiveWhere "ask first" comes from, and when it is wrong4 min▶

Newsrooms have written this way for a century - the inverted pyramid, most important thing first, so an editor can cut from the bottom without losing the story. Military briefing has the same rule under the name BLUF, bottom line up front. Both exist because a reader who stops early must still get the important part.

Three situations where it is genuinely wrong:

  • When the ask needs consent you have not earned yet. Asking a stranger for something significant in sentence one reads as a demand. One line of who-you-are first, then the ask.
  • When you are persuading rather than informing. If the reader will reject the conclusion on sight, the case has to come before it. That is a genuinely different shape, and it is the second skeleton below.
  • When the news is bad and the person is not expecting it. Leading with the ask can read as cold. Even then you get one sentence of framing, not four paragraphs - and session 6 is entirely about this case.

Note what is not on that list: "when the topic is complicated". Complexity is the strongest argument for putting the ask first, because the reader needs to know what they are reading toward before they can follow any of it.

Part 2 · covers the skeletons

Three skeletons, and one of them fits

7 min live

You do not need a library of formats. You need three, chosen by what you want to happen, and the choice takes about four seconds once you know them.

LiveThe buried lede, with a number on it4 min▶

"This feels a bit long" is an opinion and somebody can argue with it. "Your ask is in sentence 4 of 4" is a fact, and it ends the conversation.

That is what session 4's scorer measures, and it is worth understanding tonight because it changes how you edit. Daybreak's original update:

The trap, spelled out

Splitting long sentences is good editing. Done without moving the ask, it takes Daybreak's message from sentence 4 of 4 to sentence 9 of 9 - now there are nine places to stop reading instead of four. Structure before style, always. Session 4 lets you watch this happen on the meter.

"Following on from the last steering committee, I wanted to circle back and provide a holistic update on the state of play... a number of items were flagged... additional complexity was introduced... going forward we will continue to monitor and it would be appreciated if you could let us know whether you would like us to proceed with the reduced scope or push the date."

Ask in sentence 4 of 4. The founder reads two lines on her phone, sees no decision, and files it. This has now happened three times.

"It would be appreciated if you could let us know whether you would like us to proceed with the reduced scope or push the date. A number of items were flagged... additional complexity was introduced by the payments integration."

Ask in sentence 1 of 4, and the phrasing is still bad - that is session 4's job. But the founder now knows in one line that a decision is wanted from her. Structure first, then words.

Build-along

Restructure yours, without rewriting a word

★ 22 min · everyone builds

The constraint makes the point: you may move blocks and delete blocks, but you may not rewrite a sentence. Anything you gain tonight is gained by order alone.

Number your sentences. Literally. Then find the one that contains the ask and write down "N of M". That number is your before-reading, and you will want it later.

Choose a skeleton. Decision, persuasion or update. Choose by what you want to happen, not by what feels like the right format for this kind of email.

Move the ask to sentence one. Cut and paste. Do not improve it on the way past - you will get to that.

Delete the warm-up. The opening paragraph that recaps what they already know. It was for you, not for them. Daybreak's is 46 words.

Renumber and check. New "N of M". If the ask is not in sentence 1 or 2, something is still sitting above it that should not be.

Number the sentences in your own message. On paper, in a doc, however. Write down where the ask is: sentence N of M. Almost nobody has ever done this to their own writing and the number is usually worse than expected.

Pick your skeleton in four seconds. Want a decision? First one. Need to change a mind that is already made up? Second. Keeping somebody informed? Third - and check you have a "so what", because most updates stop at "what".

Move the ask, and nothing else. Resist rewriting. The discipline matters: you want to know what order alone is worth, and if you rewrite at the same time you will never find out.

Delete your warm-up paragraph. Read the first paragraph and ask what the reader learns from it that they did not know. If the answer is nothing, it goes. This is the single most deletable thing in professional writing.

Now try the trap deliberately. Take your original message and split every long sentence, without moving the ask. Recount. The ask position gets worse. This is why editing for clarity before fixing structure can leave a message harder to act on than it started.

Write your one line of "so what". Whatever the skeleton, there must be a sentence that says what this means for them. Not what happened - what it does to their week, their board meeting, their decision.

Grade the work below. Halfway. Three sessions in, no rewriting done yet, which is exactly where you should be.

If you take one thing from tonight. Structure before style. Moving one paragraph is worth more than an hour of careful editing, and editing first can actively make things worse - sentence 4 of 4 becoming sentence 9 of 9 is not a hypothetical, it is what the scorer does next session.

Homework

Before session 4

25 min
  1. Restructure two more real messages by moving and deleting only. Note the before and after ask position for each.
  2. Find a message where ask-first would be wrong. One of the three exceptions. Write down which, and what you would do instead.
  3. Audit your last update for a "so what". Is there a sentence about what it means for the reader? Add one if not.
  4. Run the trap on purpose once. Split sentences without moving the ask, and watch the position get worse. Seeing it on your own text is worth more than reading about it.
  5. Bring your restructured message to session 4. You will paste it into the scorer, and knowing its structure is already fixed means the number you get is about the words.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · You split every long sentence in your message for clarity, and leave the ask where it was. What happened to how findable the ask is?

Splitting is good editing and it is not a substitute for structure. Position is measured relative to the whole, so adding sentences above the ask pushes it down. Structure first, then style - and session 4 lets you watch the number move.

2 · When is putting the ask first genuinely the wrong move?

Complexity is the strongest argument for ask-first - the reader needs to know what they are reading toward. The real exceptions are persuasion, an ask you have not yet earned, and unexpected bad news. Even then it is one sentence of framing, not four paragraphs.

3 · Which part is missing from almost every real status update?

Most updates report events accurately and leave the reader to work out whether any of it affects them. Some will. Most will not, because they are busy - and the ones who do not are exactly the ones you needed to reach.
