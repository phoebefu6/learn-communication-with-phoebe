The spoken version

Everything from session 4 disappears in a room. Nobody can re-read your sentence, nobody can skim to the bottom, and the person you are talking to will interrupt you eleven seconds in. Part 0

Where we are

A message that scores 100 on paper is not automatically one that survives being said. The skeleton carries over. Almost nothing else does.

🎫 Clear all three quiz questions to stamp your passport Live = presented in the 45 minutes Self-study = the full depth, after class

★ What you walk out with tonight. A rehearsed opening line, a ninety-second version of your message, a plan for the interruption you know is coming, and one deliberate silence you have actually practised.

LiveThe one fact everything else follows from2 min▶

A room cannot re-read. That single constraint produces every difference between the written and spoken versions of the same message:

  • A number they miss is gone, so important numbers get said twice, deliberately, in different words.
  • A sentence with three clauses is unfollowable, because they cannot go back to the start of it.
  • Structure has to be announced rather than implied. "Two things, then what I need from you" is signposting, and on paper it would be clutter.
  • They will interrupt, which is not rudeness. It is the only editing tool available to a listener.
Part 1 · covers the opening

The first fifteen seconds decide the next ten minutes

6 min live

"So, a bit of context first" is the most common opening in professional life and it is where the room is lost. Not because context is bad, but because you have just told everyone this will take a while and they do not yet know why they should let it.

LiveThe opening line, in three parts4 min▶
  • The ask. Same rule as session 3, and it matters more out loud, because there is no scrolling to the bottom. "I need a decision from you today."
  • The shape. How many things, and how long. "Two options, ninety seconds." This is the single most underused sentence in meetings - it tells people how to listen and it stops them budgeting for the worst case.
  • Then stop. Do not roll straight into the detail. A half-second gap lets them accept the frame, and occasionally they will say "actually I already know, just tell me which one you recommend", which saves you eighty of your ninety seconds.

Write your opening line down and say it out loud three times before the meeting. Not the whole thing - just the first sentence. Almost all of the nervous rambling people do in openings is the sound of composing while speaking, and it stops the moment the first sentence is automatic.

"So, a bit of context first - you will remember at the last steering we talked about the subscription work, and there were a few dependencies, and what has happened since is that the payments integration turned out to be..."

At second eleven the founder interrupts with "sorry, is this the thing that is late?" You now answer that, out of order, defensively, and the ninety seconds you planned becomes six minutes in which nobody decides anything.

"I need a decision from you today. Two options, ninety seconds. We can ship on 18 September with pause only, or hold until 9 October and include skip and frequency."

At second eleven the founder is thinking about which one she wants, because she already knows what she is being asked. If she interrupts now, it is with a question about the options, which is exactly the conversation you came for.

Part 2 · covers the room

Cutting for the ear, the interruption, and the pause

7 min live
LiveThe interruption is the real question4 min▶

When somebody interrupts, they have told you the most useful thing you will hear all meeting: what they actually want to know. The instinct is to defend the plan - "I will come to that" - and it is almost always the wrong move.

  • Answer it, then return. "It is - three weeks, and the reason is one thing. Shall I do the options first or the reason first?" You have answered, and handed them the running order, which is theirs anyway.
  • If it comes twice, your structure is wrong. Two people interrupting at the same point is not two rude people, it is a signpost you failed to put up.
  • "I will come to that" costs more than it saves. They stop listening and hold the question, which means the next ninety seconds are spent talking to somebody who is rehearsing rather than hearing.
The exception

If the interruption is a different topic rather than an earlier one - a genuine derail - it is fair to park it: "Good question, and it is a different one. Can I finish this in thirty seconds and come to it?" Naming the thirty seconds is what makes that acceptable.

LiveSilence, on purpose4 min▶

Two places where a deliberate pause does more than any word you could put there:

  • After a number. "Three weeks late." Then nothing, for two seconds. The number lands rather than being buried under the next clause, and this is the one people find hardest because the silence feels like it needs filling.
  • After the ask. You have asked for a decision. Stop. Whoever speaks next has to engage with it, and if you keep talking that person is you, and you will start softening the ask you spent four sessions building.

The reason silence feels so long is that two seconds of it feels like six to the person who caused it and like two to everybody else. That is worth knowing, because it means your instinct about when a pause is getting awkward is systematically wrong in one direction.

Practical version: after you deliver your ask, count to three in your head before saying anything else. That is it. It is the single highest-return thing in this session and it costs nothing but nerve.

Self-studyNerves, honestly3 min▶

Two things that are true and mostly unhelpful: everybody gets nervous, and the audience cannot see it as much as you think. Here is what actually reduces it:

  • Rehearse the first sentence until it is automatic. Most visible nerves happen in the opening, because you are composing and speaking at once while adrenaline is peaking. Remove the composing and the opening steadies, and the rest usually follows.
  • Know your one sentence. If you lose your place, you can always return to the ask. Having somewhere to go is what stops a stumble becoming a spiral.
  • Slow the first ten seconds deliberately. Nervous speech is fast speech. You cannot control the adrenaline; you can control the pace of one rehearsed sentence, and the pace is what the room reads.
  • Notes are not a failure. One card with your opening line, your three points and your ask. Reading the first sentence off a card is not weak, it is what a person who prepared looks like.

What does not help: being told to relax, imagining the audience differently, or being told nobody can tell. Preparation is the intervention that reliably works.

Build-along

Ninety seconds, out loud, twice

★ 22 min · everyone builds

This one is uncomfortable and it is the session people report back on most. You need a voice recorder - your phone is fine - and somewhere you can talk.

Opening. Write the first sentence: ask plus shape. "I need a decision today. Two options, ninety seconds." Say it out loud three times until it is automatic.

Cut. Take your 100-point written version and remove everything that is detail rather than decision. A spoken message carries roughly three things. Offer the rest: "the full breakdown is in the doc."

Twice. Pick the one number that matters and say it two ways. "Three weeks late - that is 18 September becoming 9 October."

Interrupt. Write down the interruption you know is coming, and your one-line answer plus return. You almost always know what it will be, and having the sentence ready is the difference between answering and floundering.

Pause. Mark two places in your script where you will stop: after the number, after the ask. Actually mark them. Unmarked pauses do not happen.

Record yourself saying the whole thing, once, unrehearsed. Ninety seconds. Do not skip this because it is unpleasant - the unrehearsed version is your baseline and you will not believe the improvement without it.

Listen back and count three things: how long before you say the ask, how many filler words in the first fifteen seconds, and whether you paused anywhere at all. Most people's answers are: forty seconds, six, and no.

Write and rehearse the opening line only. Three times out loud. Not the whole message - just the first sentence, until you are not composing it while saying it.

Cut to three things. If your spoken version has five points, the room will retain two of them and they will not be the two you would have chosen. Pick the three, offer the rest.

Mark your two pauses in the script. Literally write "STOP" after the number and after the ask. Then record again and see whether you actually did it - most people do not, first time.

Rehearse the interruption. Say your one-line answer plus return out loud: "It is - three weeks. Shall I do the options first or the reason first?" Having that ready converts your worst moment into a normal one.

Record the second version and compare. Same content, same person, ten minutes apart. The difference is entirely preparation of the first sentence and two marked silences.

If you take one thing from tonight. After you deliver the ask, count to three before saying anything else. It will feel like six seconds. It is two, and it is the difference between a decision and a discussion.

Homework

Before session 6

20 min plus one meeting
  1. Use a real opening line in a real meeting. Ask plus shape, rehearsed. One meeting. Note what happens to the first minute.
  2. Do the count-to-three once after asking for something, and write down what the other person did with the silence.
  3. Catch yourself saying "I will come to that." Answer instead, then return. Note whether the meeting got shorter.
  4. Record one ninety-second version of something you actually have to say this week. Listen once. Delete it if you like - the listening is the exercise.
  5. Bring the hard version to session 6. The message where it is bad news, or where somebody is going to disagree with you. That is the last session and it is the one this whole course was building toward.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Somebody interrupts your update eleven seconds in. What is the best move?

The interruption tells you what they actually want to know, which is the most useful information available. "I will come to that" leaves them rehearsing their question instead of listening. Answering and then offering the order gives them what they need without losing your structure.

2 · Why say the important number twice, in different words?

Every difference between written and spoken versions comes from that one constraint. On paper the repetition would be a flaw. Out loud, "three weeks late - that is 18 September becoming 9 October" is the difference between a number heard and a number retained.

3 · You have just asked for a decision. What should you do next?

Filling your own silence is how a clear ask turns back into a discussion, because the person softening it is always you. The pause feels roughly three times longer to the person who created it than to anybody else, which is why the instinct to fill it is unreliable.
